# Dignity and inclusion

The likeliest way for Escudo to fail has nothing to do with technology. It's
someone on the kitchen floor, with a working button in reach, deciding not to
press it — because it's late, because it's probably nothing, because she doesn't
want to be a nuisance.

## Not a caseload

It's easy to build a system that splits a village into the people who help and
the people who are helped. That split is exactly what makes the button
unpressable: nobody wants to be a permanent item on someone else's list.

So everyone in the network is a member of it, and **the alarm is for what you
see, not only for what happens to you**. The neighbour at her window all day, who
notices the van that's been round twice, is the best-placed member of the Red
Escudo, not a beneficiary of it.

## No register of the vulnerable

Escudo stores a name and a way to reach it. It does not record who is frail, who
lives alone, who fell last year.

Partly that's law — health and vulnerability data is special-category under the
GDPR. Mostly it's dignity. A village of two hundred already knows who needs an
eye kept on them, and it knows it the way neighbours know things, not the way a
database knows things. Keeping an eye on
someone is an arrangement between two people, not an entry against a name.

## Built for the worst moment

- **One press.** No menus, no unlocking, no choosing.
- **Always in the same place** — the
  [buttons on the phone]({{< relref "/technology/telegram/the-group-and-the-button" >}})
  never move; a wall button is screwed to the wall.
- **It answers back.** A message, a light, a beep. Pressing a panic button and
  hearing nothing is frightening.
- **Nothing to install, nothing to log into.**
- **For those a phone can't serve, no phone** — a button by the bed or a pendant,
  no screen at all
  ([phones and devices]({{< relref "/technology/devices" >}})).

## False alarms are the price

Every alert network produces them. The false alarms aren't the problem — treating
them as one is. A single embarrassed apology teaches everybody watching to
hesitate next time, and hesitation is what kills people. So cancelling is one
button, the record stays without drama, and the convention is "false alarm, stand
down" and nothing more
([how we handle them]({{< relref "/protocols/testing-and-false-alarms" >}})).

## Loneliness is the other half

A regular check — a call, a knock, a wave from the gate — puts a person in front
of someone who might otherwise speak to nobody for days. It's also a tripwire:
**nobody answering is itself the alert**, which catches the one case no button
can.

It's the cheapest and most valuable thing a village can organise, and it needs
no technology at all.
