# What the evidence says

Most schemes like this quote the one study that flatters them. Here is the whole
picture, including the parts that don't.

## The optimistic case

- **Tilburg, 2015.** Across 35 Dutch areas that started WhatsApp prevention
  groups, monthly burglaries fell by half — from about 60 to about 30. The
  researchers said themselves they hadn't expected a fall that large.
- **A national trend the insurers noticed.** Dutch home burglaries fell 18% in
  2015 against 2014, and the insurance body linked part of it to the spread of
  neighbourhood groups.
- **The systematic review.** The Campbell review behind the College of Policing's
  toolkit finds Neighbourhood Watch is *associated with* reduced crime — while
  admitting it's hard to say how it works.

## The sceptical case

- **Later Dutch research undoes much of it.** Follow-up studies report that
  neighbourhood WhatsApp groups do **not** demonstrably prevent local crime.
  Across four studied towns, a shared "suspicious situation" led to an arrest in
  exactly **one** case.
- **Participants can feel worse.** Rotterdam research (2018–2022) found many
  groups had no measurable effect on objective safety, and that members often
  felt *less* safe than non-members. A constant drip of "suspicious" messages
  manufactures anxiety, and breeds false accusations and suspicion of outsiders.
- **Selection effects.** Watch schemes cluster in wealthier, lower-crime
  neighbourhoods — some of the apparent effect is who joins, not what joining
  does.

## The part that isn't in doubt

**[GoodSAM]({{< relref "goodsam" >}}).** Thousands of cardiac arrests, measured
against survival to hospital discharge, with a clear result: getting a nearby
volunteer there before the ambulance saves lives. It's the one place this field
has hard outcome data, and it's about **response, not surveillance**.

Alongside that, the softer findings hold up well: faster reporting, deterrence
from visible signage, stronger cohesion, and — from the Spanish rural
experience — regular contact with isolated elders that reduces both danger and
loneliness.

## What we conclude

**Crime reduction is a plausible side effect, not the promise.** The reliable
wins are speed, deterrence, cohesion and less isolation, so those are what Escudo
is built and measured on.

The design follows directly from the sceptical half:

- **Low volume, tightly scoped.** Alerts and organising who goes — not a chat
  channel ([why]({{< relref "/technology/telegram" >}})).
- **No suspect photos, no ethnicity, ever**
  ([the rule]({{< relref "/protocols/response" >}})).
- **Cheap false alarms**, so nobody hesitates
  ([how]({{< relref "/protocols/testing-and-false-alarms" >}})).
- **Neighbours checking on neighbours**, because that's the intervention with
  the best risk-to-benefit ratio of the lot — and the one a village needs no
  protocol from us to run.

## Measure it

Three numbers, honestly recorded, beat any claim on this page:

1. **How long** from alarm to the first neighbour arriving.
2. **How many false alarms**, and whether people still press the button.
3. **Whether residents feel safer** — asked directly, once a year.

If the second and third go the wrong way, the design is wrong and should change.
Full detail and sources are in the
[landscape survey](https://github.com/jpincas/escudo/tree/main/research) in the
project repository.
