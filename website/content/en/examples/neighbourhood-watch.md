Started in 1982 in a Cheshire village, and now the largest community safety
network in England and Wales: around **1.4 million households** on its alert
database and **65,000 volunteers**. Forty years is a long time for a voluntary
scheme to survive, and that's the interesting part.

## How it works

Residents form a scheme for their street. A volunteer coordinator liaises with
the local police, passes on alerts, and keeps the group going. Members watch out
for each other, report what they see, mark property and improve home security.

Two structural details stand out:

- **Schemes are owned and run by residents.** The police support them; they don't
  own them. There's no obligation to register with anyone.
- **Members do not patrol or intervene.** They're eyes and ears. Everything else
  goes to the police.

The national body, the Neighbourhood & Home Watch Network, provides the branding,
the signage and the alert system — but a scheme is its street, not its head
office.

## What we take

- **The ownership model**, almost unchanged: residents run it, authorities
  support it. It's the whole of
  [horizontal, not top-down]({{< relref "/philosophy/horizontal-not-top-down" >}}).
- **Signage as the visible half of the scheme.** The sign on the lamppost does
  work that no message ever does — it tells everyone, including the person
  casing the street, that this place is organised.
  [Ours]({{< relref "/resources/signs-and-stickers" >}}).
- **Recognisability across places.** A Watch sign means the same thing in every
  town. A Red Escudo should mean the same thing in every village.

## What we leave

- **The crime-only framing.** Watch is a crime prevention movement. Most of what
  goes wrong in a village of two hundred
  [isn't crime]({{< relref "/how-it-works/types-of-emergency" >}}).
- **Passive membership.** A Watch member is largely someone who receives
  bulletins. A Red Escudo member has said they'll get out of bed.
- **Its known weaknesses**, which the research is candid about: schemes cluster
  in wealthier, low-crime areas, and both "curtain-twitching" and discrimination
  are real risks. Our answer is the
  [Protocol]({{< relref "/protocols" >}}).
