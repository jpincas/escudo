# Choosing one, with the person

**Start with the phone they already own.** Buy a device only when there's a
reason a phone won't do — it has to be worn, or it has to work when the person is
unconscious, or the hands won't manage a keypad.

## The requirement, in a shop

One line, and it's the only thing Escudo asks:

> **It must be able to call a stored number.**

That is genuinely the whole specification. It rules almost nothing out, which is
the point: the person picks the shape that suits them rather than the shape a
committee bought in bulk.

Anything advertised as *teleasistencia*, *telealarma* or a *botón SOS* will meet
it. So will an ordinary phone. What to check on the box is narrow:

- **It stores several numbers and dials them in order.** The Escudo number goes
  first, family after.
- **It takes an ordinary SIM.** Some kits are sold locked to a subscription
  service; those are a different product, with a monthly bill and a call centre.
  Escudo doesn't need either.
- **One button raises the alarm.** Not two presses, not a press-and-hold with a
  timeout, not a menu. Anything more than one press fails the person on the
  floor.

## The shapes, and what each is good for

| | What it is | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **A phone they already own** | A dumbphone or an unused smartphone, registered as an alarm number | Anyone who carries a phone and can press one key. Costs nothing |
| **A base unit with a pendant** | A mains box in the house, a button worn round the neck | Someone mostly at home. Hands-free speech at several metres is the big advantage |
| **A GPS pendant** | A worn button with its own SIM | Someone who goes out — to the fields, to the allotment, walking |
| **A watch** | Worn, often with fall detection | Someone who won't wear a pendant. Some people won't, and that decides it |

**Fall detection and voice activation come free where a device has them.** Fall
detection raises the alarm with nobody pressing anything, which covers the case
this layer exists for. Voice activation answers a shout from the floor when the
pendant is on the bedside table. Nothing in Escudo depends on either, so treat
them as a bonus rather than a reason to spend more.

## Chosen with the person, never about them

This is the part that isn't a technical decision.

No list of the vulnerable, no committee deciding who is frail. A device comes out
of an ordinary conversation — usually out of
[Telegram onboarding]({{< relref "/protocols/onboarding-telegram" >}}), when it's
honest that a phone app isn't the answer for this person.

And it happens **one at a time, for years**. This is never a village-wide
installation day. Somebody's sight goes, somebody has a fall and doesn't want to
be alone again, somebody's hands stop managing a touchscreen. Whatever the
village does has to be worth doing for one house on a Tuesday.

**Escudo doesn't sell, choose or recommend hardware.** Show somebody what the
requirement means in a shop, then leave the choice with them. A device the person
picked is a device the person will wear.

## What a device costs

Roughly €99 for a mains base unit with a pendant, less for a plain GPS pendant,
more for a watch with fall detection. Plus a prepaid Spanish SIM, around €10 a
year.

**The family pays.** That's deliberate: it keeps the village out of deciding who
deserves one, and it keeps Escudo out of owning a fleet it would have to manage.
Nobody is ever excluded for it either — the middle door, registering a phone
somebody already owns, costs nothing at all and covers most people.

## A word on the cheap kits

The €99 units sold online are white-labelled: no manufacturer, no model number,
and a barcode the reseller reuses across products. They work, and they meet the
requirement. But there is no brand to stand behind them, no firmware updates, and
the instructions are usually a bad translation.

That's fine for a family buying one with their eyes open. It isn't something to
put in a proposal or recommend from a website, so this page doesn't name one.
